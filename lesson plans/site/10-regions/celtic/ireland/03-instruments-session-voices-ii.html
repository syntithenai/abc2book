Irish Instruments — Session Voices II

Track: regions | Tier: 3 | Difficulty: 5/10 Prerequisites: regions-celtic-ireland-02-instruments-voices-i Tags: ireland, bodhrán, guitar, bouzouki, banjo, mandolin, concertina, accordion, harmonica, session-roles

Overview

This lesson covers instruments that entered Irish sessions across different centuries — bodhrán, bones, guitar, bouzouki, mandolin, tenor banjo, concertina, button accordion, and harmonica. Some are indigenous or long-rooted (frame drum, bones); others arrived through diaspora, jazz age, or 1960s–70s fusion (guitar, bouzouki, banjo in trad lineup). All are now part of the soundscape from Miltown Malbay to Melbourne.

The organising mistake to avoid is sorting them into rigid “rhythm” and “melody” columns. In Clare, the button accordion often leads the tune. Tenor banjo doubles fiddle melody an octave up while adding percussive attack. Guitar supplies harmony but may introduce counter-melody. Bodhrán marks pulse — yet overplaying turns it into a tempo dictator, the most common session faux pas. Concertina and accordion share bellows and free reeds but occupy different regional niches. Good musicianship means listening and role flexibility, not permanent labels.

Read Traditional Voices I for fiddle, flute, whistle, pipes, and harp. Together, the two lessons map a typical pub ensemble without pretending every county sounds the same.

Session roles overlap

Sessions are social contracts. Instruments negotiate leadership tune by tune — not by category.

Overlap examples:

  • Accordion and concertina both use bellows; either may play unison melody with fiddle or rest during song.
  • Banjo and mandolin share plectrum attack; banjo often octaves fiddle while mandolin chops chords.
  • Guitar and bouzouki both supply harmony; bouzouki rings longer on open strings; guitar caps for key change.
  • Bodhrán should follow melody phrasing — if it leads, the session feels like a drum circle, not trad.

Who starts the tune? Watch that, not the instrument chart.

Bodhrán

The bodhrán (pronounced roughly BO-rawn) is a single-headed frame drum, typically 35–45 cm in diameter, with a wooden rim and goatskin or synthetic head. The player strikes with a tipper (cipín — a short beater, straight or curved) and alters tone by pressing the non-striking hand inside the skin against the rim. Wikipedia and ethnomusicologists debate whether the bodhrán is ancient or largely a nineteenth-century southwest Irish development; either way, its session prominence exploded in the twentieth-century revival, especially after ensemble experiments legitimised frame drum in trad bands.

Technique, posture, and ornament

The player usually sits, drum held vertically on the knee or angled inward. Tipper technique varies: Kerry style may use rapid wrist strokes; West Limerick players favour smoother ride patterns; some use double-ended tippers for roll effects. The inside hand is the secret: pressing toward the rim raises pitch and dampens overtones; a light touch leaves the skin boomy; a firm press yields dry, tight clicks. Good bodhrán playing mirrors melody phrasing — accenting reel lift on beats two and four, jig pulse in groups of three — rather than metronomic thumping.

Ornament on bodhrán means timbral variation: sliding the inside hand while striking, rim shots, or pausing during tune transitions. Less is often more.

Session and band role

Bodhrán supplies pulse and colour. It should enter after melody establishes tempo — experienced players listen before striking. In bands (The Chieftains, De Dannan, Lúnasa), bodhrán adds drive without replacing fiddle rhythm. In sessions, one bodhrán is a common etiquette rule; two uncordinated drums fight the tune.

Seán Ó Riada (1931–1971) placed bodhrán in Ceoltóirí Chualann arrangements — controversial then, influential forever. Ó Riada treated Irish ensemble as orchestration, not accidental pub volume; bodhrán gained legitimacy beside harp, pipes, and fiddle on stage. Memorable detail: Before Ó Riada, some trad purists saw bodhrán as inferior or modern; after Ceoltóirí Chualann and The Chieftains, it became iconic.

Notable players: Mel Mercier, Colm Murphy, John Joe Kelly (Lúnasa) — each with distinct touch (Mercier’s Clare sensitivity, Kelly’s athletic drive). Ronan Ó Snodaigh (Kíla) experiments with multicultural frame-drum vocabulary while rooted in Irish pulse — reminder that bodhrán technique continues to evolve beyond revival orthodoxy.

Manufacturing and maintenance

Modern bodhráns often feature mechanical tuners around the rim — players adjust head tension for humidity and room temperature. Goatskin heads respond to weather; synthetic heads stabilise touring conditions. Tippers range from hot-rod thin (bright attack) to bulb ends (softer thump). Beginners obsess over drum diameter; teachers insist inside-hand skill matters more than brand.

Regional associations

Bodhrán appears nationwide in revival sessions; Kerry, Clare, and West Limerick claim strong teaching lineages. Miltown Malbay summer school courses include bodhrán alongside pipes and fiddle. Manufacture thrives — Paraic McNeela, Seamus O’Kane, and other makers serve global demand.

Historical notes

Older museum examples sometimes show scarf-joined wooden frames — pre-industrial workshop construction. Mid-twentieth-century revival transformed bodhrán from occasional folk prop to session default. Critique persists: over-loud bodhrán remains the cliché session complaint. The instrument rewards restraint.

Bones

Bones are paired sticks — wood, bone, or synthetic — held in one hand and clicked rhythmically. They are idiophones: the sound is the material itself, not a membrane or string. Irish and Appalachian traditions share bones ancestry; African-American bones virtuosity influenced American folk percussion. In Ireland, bones accompany house dance, lilting, and pub sessions where a drummer is absent.

Technique and cultural context

The player curls fingers around one stick; the second stick strikes it with wrist rotation — fast triplets and reel pulse possible at expert level. Bones are loud for their size — portable rhythm for kitchens and travelling players. Technique overlaps with spoons and castanets in other folk cultures.

Session role

Bones mark rhythm; they do not lead melody. In mixed sessions they are rare but welcome when subtle — clicking along jigs or reels without drowning fiddle. Lilting (wordless mouth music) sometimes pairs with bones for dance instruction.

Famous players and historical notes

Bones lack the star cult of fiddle or pipes, but percussion traditions appear in field recordings and vaudeville-era Irish-American acts. Memorable detail: Bones prove rhythm need not mean bodhrán — useful when session etiquette limits drums.

Regional associations

Bones appear sporadically nationwide — less institutionalised than bodhrán in Comhaltas exams. Ulster and diaspora scenes preserve bones in informal settings.

Famous players and session etiquette

Percy Danforth (American) influenced global bones pedagogy; Irish players often learn through informal mentorship rather than Fleadh categories. In sessions, bones work best when one percussion voice is active — if bodhrán plays, bones usually rest. The combination can work in stage shows with arranged dynamics; in pubs it risks rhythmic clutter.

Guitar

The guitar is a relative newcomer to Irish trad — widespread in sessions only from the 1960s–70s folk revival onward — yet now anchors many pub lineups and bands. Players favour DADGAD tuning (D–A–D–G–A–D) or standard tuning with capo to match fiddle keys (D, G, A, E minor). Strumming and picking outline modal harmony without cluttering melody ornaments.

Technique and cultural context

DADGAD tuning — associated with Dáithí Spillane’s circle, Mícheál Ó Domhnaill, and Celtic guitar globally — yields open chords and ringing drones suited to Irish modes. Capo at second fret puts standard chord shapes in D session key. Right-hand patterns follow reel (four-beat) and jig (six-beat) lift; the guitar marks harmonic centre rather than soloing extensively.

Ornament is sparse compared to fiddle: occasional hammer-ons in picking intros, counter-melody in duet albums. Classical guitar technique appears in arranged repertoire but pub sessions favour rhythm support.

Session and band role

Guitar accompanies — rarely starts a tune alone in pure session culture. In bands (Bothy Band, Altan, Planxty descendants), guitar interacts with fiddle, flute, and voice. A good session guitarist listens for key changes between tunes in a set and drops out during unaccompanied song.

Famous players

Paul Brady (born 1947), Strabane, County Tyrone, began as a fiddle and whistle prodigy before guitar defined his career. Welcome Here Kind Stranger (1978) — especially Arthur McBride — set a gold standard for Irish fingerstyle accompaniment. Memorable detail: Brady’s DADGAD voicings on ballads feel orchestral without drums — harmony as storytelling.

Arty McGlynn (1944–2014), Omagh, was a guitarist’s guitarist — session work with Van Morrison, Dónal Lunny, and trad bands. His clean voicings and swing supported melody without competing. Memorable detail: McGlynn bridged showband, country, and trad — typical of Northern musicians’ versatility.

Dennis Cahill partners with Martin Hayes on lyrical East Clare duets; John Doyle (formerly Solas) advances picking and composition in Irish-American scenes.

Mícheál Ó Domhnaill (1951–2006), Donegal, with Bothy Band and duets with Kevin Burke, defined open-chord guitar behind fiddle — listen to Promenade for space and dialogue rather than chord wall. Memorable detail: Ó Domhnaill’s accompaniments breathe with fiddle phrasing; he did not strum uniformly through every bar.

Regional associations

Guitar is ubiquitous in urban and diaspora sessions — Dublin, Galway, Boston, Chicago. Regional accent matters less than for fiddle; personal style (Brady vs McGlynn vs Doyle) dominates.

Historical notes

Michael Coleman’s occasional 1930s recordings with tenor guitarist “Whitey” Andrews show guitar in Irish dance music decades before the folk boom — exception, not rule. 1960s–70s revival (pubs, festivals, LP recordings) made guitar expected. Debate continues: purists once resisted guitar as non-traditional; practical session culture overruled.

Irish bouzouki

The Irish bouzouki adapts the Greek bouzouki (long neck, paired strings, flat back) for trad accompaniment and counter-melody. Andy Irvine and Dónal Lunny popularised the instrument in Planxty and 1970s ensembles; Irish makers developed flat-backed, wider-necked variants optimised for open chords and modal tuning.

Technique and cultural context

Tuning varies — often GDAD or variants — with octave pairs on courses. Right hand strums or picks arpeggios; left hand forms open chords in D mixolydian, A dorian, and related modes. Sustain is longer than mandolin chop; bouzouki fills harmonic space under fiddle reels.

Session and band role

Bouzouki accompanies primarily; counter-melody lines answer fiddle in bands (Planxty, Mozaik, Usher’s Island). In sessions, one bouzouki plus one guitar requires coordination — not identical chord voicings stomping the same frequencies.

Famous players

Andy Irvine (born 1942), London-born, brought Balkan and Greek influences to Irish ensemble after time in Eastern Europe. His bouzouki on Raggle Taggle Gypsy and Planxty catalogue defined ringing open-string accompaniment. Memorable detail: Irvine plays mandolin and harmonica too — a model of multi-instrument trad musicianship.

Dónal Lunny (born 1947), Tullamore, is a founding architect of Irish fusion ensemble — Planxty, Moving Hearts, Coolfin. His bouzouki and bodhrán work emphasise rhythmic architecture. Memorable detail: Lunny helped design Irish bouzouki maker specs — player as instrument evolution partner.

Roger McGuire, Donal Murphy, and younger players extend jazz-chord and picking vocabulary.

Bouzouki versus guitar in the same session

When both appear, division of labour matters: guitar often handles rhythmic chord changes while bouzouki sustains open drones and moving bass lines on lower strings. Clashing happens when both strum the same voicings in the same register — the session sounds muddy. Watch Planxty recordings: Lunny and Moore rarely occupy identical harmonic space. If you play one, learn to leave holes for the other.

Regional associations

Bouzouki is national and diaspora — strong wherever Planxty-influenced bands took root (Dublin, Belfast, London, Berlin trad scenes).

Historical notes

Greek bouzouki arrived via 1960s world-music curiosity; Irish adaptation is younger than fiddle but now canonical. Not medieval — honest history helps beginners place the sound.

Mandolin

The mandolin — paired strings, tuned in fifths like violin (G-D-A-E), played with plectrum — entered Irish sessions through Italian-American and bluegrass crosscurrents. Irish use emphasises chord chops on off-beats and bright counter-melody, not Bill Monroe repertoire alone.

Technique and cultural context

Chop chords — short muted strums on beats two and four — provide percussive harmony in bands. Single-note melody doubles fiddle at pitch (same tuning as violin). Tremolo appears on slow airs occasionally; dance tunes favour staccato pick strokes.

Session and band role

Mandolin supports more often than leads in pub sessions; in ceilí bands and stage ensembles it adds sparkle above guitar/bouzouki foundation. Pairs naturally with banjo (similar attack) but requires arrangement to avoid frequency clash.

Famous players

Andy Irvine doubles on mandolin; Dónal Lunny played mandolin in early groups. Barney McKenna occasionally contributed mandolin colour. Irish-American players in Solas and Cherish the Ladies use mandolin in arranged sets.

Regional associations

Less regionally fixed than accordion; common in urban and American Irish sessions.

Famous players and memorable details

Andy Irvine records mandolin on Planxty tracks — bright counterpoint under bouzouki and guitar. Barry Mitterhoff (American, Solas) brings jazz mandolin vocabulary to Irish sets without abandoning trad phrasing. Memorable detail: Mandolin chop on off-beats imitates snare role — another overlap case where a “melody” instrument supplies rhythm.

Historical notes

Mandolin was popular in late-nineteenth-century parlour music globally; Irish trad adoption is twentieth-century layer on older dance repertoire. Italian immigrant communities in America and Australia crossed paths with Irish musicians in urban melting pots — mandolin entered sessions through shared folk spaces, not a single inventor.

Four-string tenor banjo

Irish trad uses the four-string tenor banjo — not the five-string bluegrass banjo. Tuning is commonly C-G-D-A (same intervals as viola) or G-D-A-E (octave below fiddle). Played with a plectrum, it cuts through pub noise doubling fiddle reels an octave above or at pitch.

Technique and cultural context

Right hand picks alternate or triplet patterns; left hand slides and cuts imitate fiddle ornament within banjo physics. Percussive attack makes banjo both melody and rhythm — key overlap example. Mute techniques reduce ring when session dynamics require.

Session and band role

Banjo often joins after fiddle establishes tune — doubling A and B parts. In The Dubliners and The Bothy Band lineage, banjo is front-line voice. Some sessions ban banjo (rare); most welcome one banjo if player listens.

Famous players

Barney McKenna (1939–2012), Dublin, The Dubliners tenor banjoist for fifty years, popularised Irish banjo for millions. His flying triplets on The Mason’s Apron set student goals for generations. Memorable detail: McKenna’s C-G-D-A tuning and aggressive drive made banjo synonymous with Irish pub music abroad — sometimes overshadowing regional fiddle nuance.

Kieran Hanrahan, Gerry O’Connor (Tullamore), and David Carty represent Clare and national banjo schools with more fiddle-aligned ornament than McKenna’s punch.

Regional associations

Dublin showband and folk-pub legacy; Clare and Galway sessions integrate banjo alongside fiddle. Diaspora sessions (London, New York) often feature banjo prominently.

Historical notes

Banjo entered Irish music via music hall, jazz age, and American minstrel routes — historically complicated. Twentieth-century players reclaimed the instrument for trad repertoire. Four-string plectrum banjo differs from five-string Scruggs roll — important distinction for learners from bluegrass.

Concertina

The Anglo concertina — push/pull buttons sound different notes — is compact, portable, and expressive in Irish hands. English and Anglo systems differ; Irish players overwhelmingly use Anglo (often C/G layout), ornamenting like pipers or fiddlers with cuts, crans, and bellows articulation.

Technique and cultural context

Each button sounds two notes — one on push, one on pull — so direction changes become ornament opportunities. Bellows are the player’s breath: phrasing comes from expansion and compression, not only fingers. Irish concertina is legato compared to English morris tradition — long phrases on slow airs, crisp reel attack on dance tunes.

Session and band role

In Clare, West Limerick, and Connemara sessions, concertina leads tune sets as often as fiddle. In mixed lineups, concertina rests during keys that lie awkwardly on instrument layout. Noel Hill collaborations with guitar show duet potential — concertina and guitar can carry a full pub set without fiddle if repertoire aligns.

Competition stages at Fleadh Cheoil feature concertina solos where no bodhrán or guitar supports — proof the instrument is melody-complete on its own. Session players who treat concertina as “backup only” have not heard Clare leaders open reels at full tilt.

Famous players

Noel Hill (born 1958), Clare, is widely regarded as among the greatest Irish concertina players — technical precision with emotional slow airs. His recordings with Tony Linnane (fiddle) and Mícheál Ó Súilleabháin arrangements expanded concertina concert repertoire. Memorable detail: Hill’s bellows control makes silence part of phrases — not only button virtuosity.

Elizabeth Crotty (1884–1960), Kilrush, Clare, preserved older women’s concertina tradition — field recordings essential for historical style. Niall Vallely, Jack Talty, and Mick Mulcahy carry Clare and Sliabh Luachra lineages forward.

Regional associations

County Clare is concertina heartland — Kilrush, Miltown Malbay, Ennis sessions. Kerry and Cork also claim masters. Fleadh Cheoil concertina competitions draw youth nationwide.

Historical notes

Concertina arrived from England and Germany in nineteenth century; Irish players developed distinct ornament grammar. Anglo system won over English system in Ireland for melody flexibility.

Button accordion

The button accordion (“box”) uses bellows and button keyboards (not piano keys) with bass chords on the left. Irish players favour melody-forward style — bass chords sparingly compared to continental polka bands. Common tunings: B/C and C#/D — each implies different finger geography and key comfort.

Technique and cultural context

B/C tuning places many session keys (D, G, A) under cross-row fingering; C#/D tuning suits D and G melody lines with different ornament habits. Right hand plays melody with cuts and crans adapted from fiddle; left hand adds chord punctuation. Bellows direction shapes phrasing — push and pull are not interchangeable.

Session and band role

In Clare and Kerry, accordion starts reels — fiddle joins. Box carries polka and slide repertoire in Sliabh Luachra with distinctive lift. In ballad groups, accordion fills between verses. Session etiquette: one dominant box avoids harmonic mud.

Famous players

Joe Burke (born 1939), Loughrea, Galway, epitomises East Galway box — smooth, lyrical reels and masterful bellows. All-Ireland champion and teacher; memorable detail: Burke’s tone is round, not shrill — concertina-like sensitivity on accordion.

Sharon Shannon (born 1968), Clare, brought accordion to pop-folk audiences (Galway Girl) while retaining trad technique. Her speed and stage energy inspired young players; memorable detail: Shannon proved box could fill festivals without sacrificing session credentials.

Jackie Daly (Sliabh Luachra polkas), Máirtín O’Connor, Bobby Gardiner, and Tony Mac Mahon (iconic slow air box) span regional styles. Memorable detail: Mac Mahon’s sparse left hand on airs treats silence as accompaniment — lesson for guitarists who over-strum ballads.

B/C versus C#/D is not merely mechanical: B/C players often describe fluid cross-row fingering for reels in D and G; C#/D players in Kerry and Sliabh Luachra describe direct melody on the home row for polkas and slides. Listen to Jackie Daly (C#/D polkas) beside Joe Burke (B/C lyrical reels) — same instrument family, different geographic grammar.

Historical notes

Accordion arrived late nineteenth century; mass manufacture made it loud session voice where harp and pipes declined. Fleadh Cheoil accordion categories — solo, ceili band — institutionalise the instrument. Piano accordion exists but button dominates Irish trad.

Harmonica

The harmonica (mouth organ) appears in trad-adjacent pub bands, folk-rock, and solo busking — melody, fills, or blues inflection. It is not the core of every regional session (compare Clare box or Sligo fiddle), but illustrates Irish music absorbing neighbouring folk styles.

Technique and cultural context

Diatonic harmonicas in session keys (D, G) allow bend and tongue-block ornament; chromatic harmonica suits jazz crossover. Players match reel and jig breath patterns — short phrases, not endless sustain.

Session role

Harmonica fills between vocal verses in ballad groups; occasional tune lines in fusion outfits. Rare as only melody instrument in pure trad session — but not forbidden.

Famous players

Donal Lunny and Andy Irvine use harmonica on recordings; Johnny and Mickey harmonica traditions appear in showband and folk archives. Memorable detail: Harmonica signals crossover listening — American blues, Scottish folk — without leaving Irish repertoire.

Regional associations

Urban and diaspora scenes more than rural Clare pure-trad pubs.

Historical notes

Harmonica entered Irish music via radio and records; permanent guest in fusion, optional in core session. Hohner mass production put harmonicas in pockets like whistles; trad adoption stayed optional because fiddle, flute, and box already saturated melody leadership — yet harmonica remains welcome when a player respects session dynamics.

Planxty and the modern ensemble template

No survey of Session Voices II is complete without Planxty (1972–1980s, with later revivals): Christy Moore (guitar, bodhrán, voice), Andy Irvine (bouzouki, mandolin, harmonica), Dónal Lunny (bouzouki, guitar, bodhrán), Liam O’Flynn (uilleann pipes). Planxty did not invent guitar or bouzouki in Ireland, but it codified how they interact with pipes and fiddle — counter-melody, open-string ring, arranged transitions between tunes in a set.

Listen to The Well Below the Valley or Raggle Taggle Gypsy as ensemble textbooks: who enters when, how bodhrán supports without dominating, how bouzouki fills harmonic space guitar leaves empty. Memorable detail: Planxty treated Irish trad as arranged chamber music without losing dance pulse — a direct ancestor of how Lúnasa, Danú, and Goitse stage sessions today.

Moving Hearts (Lunny, rock pulse meets pipes) and The Bothy Band (banjo, flute, fiddle, guitar, bodhrán) extended the template in different directions — one toward fusion, one toward pure trad with modern ensemble clarity. Ceoltóirí Chualann (Ó Riada) preceded them; Planxty popularised the guitar-bouzouki-pipes triangle for post-1970 listeners.

Seán Ó Riada’s legacy threads through this entire lesson: bodhrán on stage, harp in ensemble, pipes as art music — Session Voices II instruments gained respect through arranged bands before they saturated pub sessions. Understanding that history explains why older players sometimes say “we didn’t always play guitar in trad” while younger players cannot imagine a session without it.

Putting the ensemble together

Picture a Clare pub: accordion launches a reel in D; fiddle joins unison; flute harmonises an octave up; concertina alternates leading if two tune players negotiate; bodhrán enters softly on the second tune; guitar outlines DADGAD chords; bouzouki rings open fifths; banjo doubles the A part an octave higher. Change county — Donegal might be fiddle-forward with fewer boxes; Sliabh Luachra pivots to polkas with accordion and fiddle; Dublin session may feature banjo and guitar prominently.

Listen for:

  1. Who starts each tune
  2. When bodhrán enters
  3. Whether guitar drops during song
  4. If banjo doubles or counters fiddle
  5. B/C vs C#/D box colour in Munster

Sorting instruments into melody and rhythm columns fails because roles shift tune by tune. The musician who listens keeps the session alive; the one who labels misses the music.

Session etiquette checklist

  1. Arrive listening — one tune minimum before joining.
  2. Match key and tempo — if you do not know the tune, play quietly or omit.
  3. One bodhrán unless invited otherwise.
  4. Guitar and bouzouki coordinate voicings — talk before the set or follow the senior player.
  5. Box and concertina — avoid duelling in the same register; trade leadership.
  6. Banjo doubles best when fiddle is secure — do not race ahead.
  7. Song sets — melody instruments drop; guitar may continue gentle accompaniment if singer welcomes it.

Diaspora sessions: same instruments, different defaults

Boston, Chicago, and London Irish sessions often feature guitar and banjo more prominently than some rural Munster pubs — diaspora lineups reflect local musician pools and venue acoustics, not a rulebook. Newfoundland accordion tradition connects to Kerry and Cork emigration; C#/D box appears in contexts rare in Dublin. When travelling, observe before assuming Clare or Sligo defaults apply.
