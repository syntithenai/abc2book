Tunes, Forms, and Style

Track: regions | Tier: 4 | Difficulty: 4/10 Prerequisites: regions-celtic-ireland-03-instruments-voices-ii Tags: ireland, jig, reel, hornpipe, polka, slide, ornamentation, sean-nós, ballad, slow-air, lilting, session

Overview

If the instrument lessons taught you who speaks in an Irish session, this lesson teaches you what language they speak. Irish traditional music is organised around dance forms — reels, jigs, hornpipes, polkas, slides, and rarer metres — each with its own pulse, tempo range, and social history. Running alongside instrumental dance music is a parallel world of song: sean-nós in Irish, narrative ballads in English, drinking songs and laments, work songs and lilting. Ornamentation turns a skeleton tune into a recognisable regional accent; sets and variation turn a single melody into a living conversation.

This lesson is long because the subject is wide. Take it in sections: learn to identify the form first (reel versus jig is the classic beginner trap), then study ornament on your own instrument, then explore song traditions as separate repertoires that sometimes share melodies with dance tunes. By the end you should be able to foot-tap confidently, name what you are hearing, and understand why a Clare polka set and a Donegal highland feel like different countries wearing the same passport.

How to listen: metre, pulse, and feel

Before memorising tune names, train your ear for metre (how beats are grouped) and feel (how those beats are articulated). Written time signatures are guides, not prisons: hornpipes may be notated in 4/4 but played with a dotted swing; sean-nós song may float across bar lines; slow airs may have almost no fixed beat at all.

A useful habit: tap your foot and say the pulse aloud. Reels want a steady ONE-two THREE-four drive. Double jigs want JIG-ged-y JIG-ged-y — two main beats per bar, each divided into three. If you tap reel rhythm to a jig, experienced players will notice immediately and the tune will feel wrong in your body even if every note is correct.

Feel also includes lift — the subtle emphasis that makes dance music bounce. Lift comes from bowing (fiddle), tonguing (flute, whistle), bellows pressure (accordion, concertina), or rhythmic articulation (banjo). It is not the same as playing louder; it is playing with the dance impulse, even when no one is dancing.

Reels

Reels are the workhorse of the Irish session. They are most often in 4/4 or notated in 2/2 (cut time) with a steady, driving pulse at moderate to fast tempo. A typical session might chain two or three reels before switching to jigs or hornpipes. Reels dominate because they suit the social energy of pub gatherings: forward motion, clear downbeats, room for melodic variation without losing the thread.

Structurally, most reels are binary — an A part and a B part, each often eight bars, repeated. Musicians learn parts first, then sets of multiple tunes. Classic examples heard everywhere include The Wind That Shakes the Barley, The Maid Behind the Bar, Drowsy Maggie, The Silver Spear, and The Musical Priest. Diaspora fiddlers from Michael Coleman to Cape Breton players have spread reel repertoire so widely that the same tune may carry different ornaments in Sligo, Donegal, and New England.

Reel bowing on fiddle often slurs across beats to create back-beat lift — a long bow stroke followed by a crisp catch. Flute and whistle players articulate with tonguing patterns that echo fiddle bowing. Banjo doubles the melody an octave higher with percussive attack. Accordion and concertina players phrase in bellows strokes that breathe with the tune.

Memorable detail: Confusing a reel with a double jig is the most common beginner error. If the room starts tapping wrong, check the metre before blaming your instrument.

Jigs: double, single, and slip

Double jigs (6/8)

Double jigs are in 6/8 time with a strong two-beat feel per bar: each beat divides into three eighth notes. They are the second most common session form after reels. Dance associations are broad — group céilí figures, solo step dance, and informal kitchen dancing all use jig rhythm.

Famous double jigs include The Swallowtail Jig, The Irish Washerwoman (often played faster and lighter than newcomers expect), The Kesh Jig, Banish Misfortune, and The Lilting Banshee. The A and B parts repeat; musicians often pair a well-known jig with a lesser-known companion in a set.

Single jigs

Single jigs share 6/8 metre but lighten the rhythmic pattern — more crotchet–quaver (quarter–eighth) motion, less of the continuous triplet stream of a double jig. They are less common in modern sessions than doubles but appear in regional repertoire and older publications. Listen for a slightly skipping quality compared with the rolling drive of a double jig.

If you transcribe a single jig from a recording, you may be tempted to write endless triplet groups because that is how double jigs look on the page. Resist: single-jig phrasing often alternates longer notes with quick catches, and fiddlers may bow the pattern differently from flute players’ tonguing. The Frost Is All Over and The Hag at the Churn are often played with single-jig feel in some regions — always verify against a local source rather than a generic tune book.

Single jigs also appear in step-dance teaching as light jigs — the same metre family with a pedagogical name. Musicians backing beginners learn that “jig” in a dance class may mean single-jig articulation even when the session next door plays doubles at full tilt.

Slip jigs (9/8)

Slip jigs use 9/8 — three beats per bar, each divided into three eighth notes. They feel lighter and more asymmetrical than double jigs, with a graceful lilt often linked to soft-shoe step dance in the competitive tradition. Hardiman the Fiddler, The Butterfly, and The Foxhunter’s Jig are slip-jig standards.

Slip jigs reward patience: the nine-beat bar can feel like it “turns” in the middle if you are used to six-beat jigs. Dancers and musicians share vocabulary here — slip-jig steps match the tune’s elongated phrase shapes.

Hornpipes

Hornpipes are usually notated in 4/4 but played with a dotted, long-short bounce — slower than reels, with a swagger sometimes linked to sailor tunes and stage hornpipe dances. Names like Off to California, The Sailor’s Hornpipe, The Boys of Bluehill, and The Harvest Home signal the type immediately to experienced players.

Regional dotting varies: Donegal fiddlers and Clare players may articulate hornpipes differently. Some sessions play hornpipes relatively straight; others lean heavily into the dotted snap. Compare recordings rather than trusting a single metronome marking.

Hornpipes suit hard-shoe step dance in competition — the dotted rhythm matches heel-and-toe percussion on the floor. Musicians accompanying feiseanna learn hornpipe tempo as a distinct category from session reels.

Polkas

Irish polkas are in 2/4 with a brisk, simple harmonic motion — not to be confused with Central European classical polkas or Oktoberfest clichés. They are strongest in Munster, especially Sliabh Luachra (the Cork–Kerry border region) and west Kerry. Polkas often appear in sets alternating with slides; dancers in set-dancing revival scenes depend on them.

Examples include Denis Murphy’s Polka, Johnny I Hardly Knew Ye (as instrumental polka sets), and numerous unnamed local polkas passed ear-to-ear. Accordion and fiddle carry the form; guitar and bouzouki outline chords in D, G, and A.

Polka rhythm is uneven in a good way — a slight lilt on the off-beat that keeps feet moving in crowded kitchens. If you play polkas like stiff march tunes, set dancers will tell you with their eyes.

Slides (12/8 tune type)

Do not confuse slides (the 12/8 tune form) with slides (the ornament — a portamento between notes). The tune type is a jig family metre concentrated in Sliabh Luachra and west Kerry. Slides feel like jigs stretched and relaxed — twelve eighth notes grouped in a distinctive pulse (often described as four beats of three, but felt differently from 6/8 doubles).

The Star Above the Garter, The Gallant Boys of Tipperary (slide settings), and local Sliabh Luachra repertoire staples belong here. Slides are session gold in Kerry and can sound unfamiliar to players who learned only from generic Dublin tune books.

Memorable detail: A musician who knows reels and double jigs but has never played a slide may still “sort of” play one — and miss the regional accent entirely. Learn at least one slide from a Sliabh Luachra recording.

Reading the session: from foot-tap to tune name

Experienced session players run a mental checklist in the first eight bars of any tune:

  1. Metre — reel, double jig, slip jig, hornpipe, polka, slide, or something rarer?
  2. Mode and key — D major, E minor, A mixolydian flavour?
  3. Regional accent — Sligo rolls, Donegal snap, Clare box phrasing?
  4. Set position — is this the opening tune or the third reel in a chain?

You do not need perfect pitch to participate; you need pattern recognition. Apps like The Session and tune databases in ABC notation help once you know the form. ABC is a text format musicians share online — one line of letters and numbers can encode a whole reel. Treat ABC as a map back to recordings, not a substitute for listening.

When someone starts a tune you almost know, wait one repetition before joining. Trad etiquette favours the listener who enters cleanly over the enthusiast who guesses wrong and drags the room off pulse.

Mazurkas and continental metres

Mazurkas appear in Irish trad as a minority form — often three beats per bar (3/4) with a characteristic accent pattern borrowed from Polish and continental dance music. They entered Irish repertoire through travelling musicians, sheet music, and cross-cultural exchange in the nineteenth century. You will not hear mazurkas every night in a Dublin session, but they appear in collections and in some regional players’ tune bags.

Irish mazurkas such as Sonny’s Mazurka or settings of continental tunes carry a different lilt from waltzes: emphasis lands on beat two or three in ways waltz players may find surprising. Treat them as a formal cousin to polkas and slides — regional spice, not session default.

Highlands, strathspeys, and Scottish overlap

Donegal and parts of the Ulster tradition share repertoire with Scottish fiddle music — highlands (sometimes called flings), strathspey-like tunes, and Scottish marches. The highland in Irish usage is a dance tune type with a dotted, Scottish snap — related to the strathspey’s long-short rhythm but with Irish local names and settings.

This is evidence of cross-channel tune exchange over centuries: fishing routes, seasonal labour, shared Gaelic culture, and later radio and records. A Donegal fiddler may play a tune recognisable in Cape Breton or Aberdeenshire with Irish bowing and ornament. Musicians who ignore this strand miss half the Donegal story.

Listen to players like John Doherty for highland repertoire; compare with Scottish sources to hear what stayed and what changed.

Ornamentation: the grammar of trad style

Ornament is not decoration sprinkled at random — it is grammar. It marks stress points, matches dance lift, and signals regional identity. Copy ornaments from specific players and recordings; a generic “Irish ornament” exercise often sounds like no place at all.

Cuts

A cut is a quick grace note above the main pitch, clipped and clean. Cuts articulate melody on whistle, flute, fiddle, and pipes. They say “this note matters” without changing the tune’s skeleton.

Rolls

Rolls flicker around a sustained note — combinations of cuts and gracenotes that vary by instrument. Fiddle rolls use bowed gracenotes; flute and whistle rolls use finger articulation; pipe rolls use chanter technique. Sligo fiddle style is famous for rolls (Michael Coleman, James Morrison); rolls are a calling card of that accent.

Trebles

On fiddle, a treble is a bowed triplet figure — three notes in the space of two, or a rhythmic triplet ornament depending on context. Trebles drive reel lift and are central to Coleman-era Sligo style. They are not the same as rolls, though listeners sometimes lump them together.

Cranning (uilleann pipes)

Cranning is a pipe-specific ornament on the chanter — a rapid alternation of gracenotes around a note when you cannot sustain pitch with vibrato (chanter sound dies if you stop articulating). Cran technique is one reason pipe melodies sound so different from fiddle transcriptions. Pipers learn crans as essential vocabulary, not optional flair.

Slides (ornament)

As an ornament, a slide is a portamento — gliding between pitches. Fiddlers use finger slides; singers use melisma. This is unrelated to the 12/8 slide tune type except in name — context tells you which is meant.

Sean-nós singing

Sean-nós (“old style”) is unaccompanied traditional singing, most often in Irish, with highly personal ornament, free rubato, and no fixed metronome. It is not one style but a family of regional approaches united by philosophy: the singer owns the song, not the other way around.

Regional styles

Connemara (Galway) sean-nós is often described as flowing, ornament-rich, with melismatic decoration and emotional dynamics. Singers like Seosamh Ó hÉanaí and Róisín Elsafty represent strands of this tradition for modern listeners.

Donegal sean-nós can sound straighter and more rhythmic to some ears — influenced by Scottish Gaelic song and Donegal’s instrumental rhythm traditions. Proinsias Ó Maonaigh and family traditions anchor Donegal sean-nós in living memory.

Munster (Cork, Kerry, Waterford) carries its own sean-nós accent — ornament choices, vowel colour, and repertoire differ from Connemara. Regional competitions and Oireachtas na Gaeilge stages have preserved songs that might otherwise survive only in fragments.

Ornament and metre

Sean-nós ornament includes melisma, pitch slides, and rhythmic displacement — the singer stretches a syllable across beats or compresses a phrase for emphasis. There is no fixed metre in the sense of dance music: the pulse follows text and emotion. Accompanying sean-nós on guitar or harp is a modern concert practice; purists associate the form with voice alone.

Repertoire and function

Songs may be lament, love song, working song, or narrative. Many sean-nós songs are poetic — dense with metaphor, local place names, and historical reference. Learning one song deeply teaches language, history, and ornament together.

Amhrán mór (“big song”) tradition and shorter amhráin (songs) coexist in Irish-language repertoire. Long narrative songs may take fifteen minutes — verse after verse of battle, exile, or betrayal. Shorter songs suit learning Irish phonetics: Trasna na dTonnta (Across the Waves) is a classroom favourite; An Poc ar Buile (The Mad Puck Goat) is comic chaos with virtuosic delivery.

Competition stages at Oireachtas na Gaeilge preserve songs that might otherwise survive only in archives. If you sing sean-nós in English by mistake, you are probably singing a ballad or folk revival arrangement — not wrong, but a different category.

Ballad culture: narrative, emigration, and politics

English-language ballads in Ireland are story songs — murder ballads, love tragedies, comic narratives, emigration tales, and political comment. They flourished in pub singing, kitchen parties, and ballad boom ensembles from the 1960s onward (The Dubliners, The Clancy Brothers, Christy Moore and later acts).

Narrative is central: verses advance plot; chorus anchors memory. The Wild Rover (temperance twist ending), The Fields of Athenry (famine-era emigration), Boolavogue (1798 rebellion), and The Town I Loved So Well (Derry, Troubles-era reflection) show how ballads carry history in singable form.

Emigration songs — Skibbereen, The Leaving of Liverpool, Thousands Are Sailing — document hunger, exile, and diaspora identity. They overlap with Irish-American repertoire; the same song may be claimed by pub singers in Dublin and Boston.

Political ballads preview a wider culture of protest song: The Rising of the Moon, James Connolly, Only Our Rivers Run Free. Musicians should know these as repertoire with context — performing them without understanding has social consequences in Irish rooms.

Ballads are not “less traditional” than sean-nós; they are a parallel oral tradition, often with older British broadside roots adapted locally.

The ballad boom and pub stages

From the 1960s, groups like The Dubliners, The Clancy Brothers and Tommy Makem, and later Planxty, The Bothy Band, and Christy Moore put narrative song in pubs, concert halls, and television. Luke Kelly’s renditions of The Town I Loved So Well and Raglan Road (poem by Patrick Kavanagh, melody traditional) showed how literary and oral traditions merge.

Pub ballad singing is participatory: the audience owns the chorus even when they do not know the verses. A good ballad singer paces story — rushing a famine narrative insults the material; dragging a comic song kills the room. Instrumentalists who accompany ballads must follow verse rhythm and leave space for consonants.

Child ballads and local variants

Collectors documented Child ballads (Francis James Child’s numbering system) in Irish settings — murder ballads, supernatural encounters, cross-dressing heroines. She Moved Through the Fair floats between air and ballad; The Unquiet Grave survives in minimalist English versions. Irish singers localise place names and political references — the same melodic skeleton, new verses for new grievances.

Drinking songs, laments, and work songs

Drinking songs (The Wild Rover, Whiskey in the Jar, Finnegan’s Wake) are communal, chorus-heavy, and designed for group participation. They are not musically subtle — they are social glue.

Laments (She Moved Through the Fair, The Lament for Limerick, sean-nós equivalents in Irish) slow the room down. Air and phrasing matter more than rhythmic drive.

Work songs — waulking songs, milling songs, sailor labour chants — survive in fragments and revival performances. They remind us trad is not only pub reels but labour rhythms tied to tasks. Sean-nós and lilting sometimes preserve work-song pulse without the original workplace.

Scottish Gaelic waulking songs (òrain luaidh) influenced Irish song research; in Ireland, spailpín (migrant labourer) songs and fishing refrains carry similar call-and-response energy. A verse might be solo; the chorus returns with collective force — the same social architecture as pub ballads, but tied to work rather than leisure.

For instrumentalists, work-song rhythm explains why some reels feel like they “pull” — the tune encodes a physical task’s repetition. When you hear older singers talk about “the time of the songs,” they often mean when work and music were not separate categories.

Culture of lyrics: language, poetry, and communal singing

Irish trad culture is bilingual in practice even when musicians speak only English. Song titles, chorus lines, and session banter mix Irish and English. Competitions at Oireachtas na Gaeilge privilege Irish-language song; pub stages may be mostly English — same island, different rooms.

Poetic forms in Irish song include structured verse types (some inherited from bardic poetry) and looser narrative verse in English ballads. Assonance, internal rhyme, and place naming anchor songs to locality — a listener from West Clare recognises different references than a Dubliner.

Communal singing shapes performance: choruses invite the room; verses may be solo. Story-telling function matters as much as melody — a song explains who we were, who we lost, and who we argue about still. Instrumental players who never learn lyrics miss half the emotional map.

Liliting (portaireacht)

Lilting (portaireacht) is wordless vocal music — the voice imitates fiddle, pipes, or accordion rhythm and melody using nonsense syllables (“diddly-dee”, “thrum-a-lum”, and endless local variants). It served house parties when no instrument was available, taught dance rhythm, and passed tunes between generations.

Lilting is not comedy — though it can be joyful. Skilled lilters like Percy French (also a songwriter) or competition lilters at fleadhanna demonstrate rhythmic precision. For learners, lilting is a pedagogy tool: if you can lilt a reel, you understand its pulse without finger technique blocking you.

Lilting connects to bones playing and dance masters who counted rhythm aloud — voice as percussion and melody together.

Slow airs

Slow airs are lyrical, often free-tempo melodies — frequently drawn from sean-nós song or lament tradition. They are not dance tunes. Putting a slow air unannounced in the middle of a reel set is a session faux pas unless the room expects it (commemoration, singer’s turn, concert programme).

Air performance favours sparse ornament, dynamic swells, and personal phrasing. Harp, fiddle, uilleann pipes, and flute each have air traditions. Danny Boy (lyric to Londonderry Air) is the famous export; Irish players also cherish The Coolin, Give Me Your Hand, and pipe airs like Tabhair dom do Lámh.

Air rhythm breathes with the phrase — metronomes are poor teachers here. Listen to pipers and fiddlers who specialise in airs; copy where they pause, not only which notes they play.

Sets, variation, and tune naming

Sets

Sessions chain tunes in sets — usually two to four tunes of the same form (three reels, two jigs) before switching metre. Sets build energy and let each musician settle into keys and ornaments. Endings matter: the last bar of a tune signals the next tune’s start; sloppy endings break dancers’ and listeners’ trust.

Variation

Variation means repeating a tune with new melodic turns, extra ornaments, or altered endings while keeping the skeleton recognisable. Coleman-era fiddle recordings are masterclasses. Variation is expected in traditional performance — playing the same A part identically three times through can sound stiff once you develop ear.

Tune naming

Oral tradition produces multiple names for one melody, one name for several unrelated tunes, and composer attribution that may be half legend. Tune books (Francis O’Neill’s collections, Breandán Breathnach’s Ceol Rince na hÉireann) standardise some names — but local sessions keep local titles.

Keys shift by instrument: fiddle in D, whistle in D, accordion in B/C layout — same tune, different finger geography. ABC notation and apps help, but ear learning remains central.

Memorable detail: Arguing about tune names is a session sport. When in doubt, ask “how do you call it?” and accept the local answer for the night.

Francis O’Neill and the printed tradition

No genres lesson is complete without Francis O’Neill (1848–1936) — Chicago police chief, collector, and publisher of Music of Ireland (1903) and Dance Music of Ireland (1907), among others. O’Neill did not invent the tunes; he captured names, settings, and variants from Irish musicians in America and linked them to a notated tradition that beginners still use.

Printed collections standardise but also freeze — a tune in O’Neill may differ from a Clare session version by an entire part. Use books as indexes to find melodies, then go to recordings for ornament and tempo. The Irish Traditional Music Archive (ITMA) in Dublin holds manuscripts, 78 rpm discs, and field recordings that complicate any single printed version.

Memorable detail: O’Neill’s Chicago network included musicians who emigrated after the famine and the Land War — the same diaspora that spread reels to recording studios and sent Coleman-style playing back to Ireland.

When forms collide: medleys, surprises, and session politics

Skilled sets change form only at boundaries — three reels, pause, two jigs. Medleys that jump reel-to-jig-to-hornpipe without warning frustrate dancers and confuse newcomers. Slow airs in dance sets require social consent — a memorial tune, a singer’s spot, a concert programme.

Key clashes between tunes in a set matter: D reel to E minor reel flows; D reel to B-flat polka may require retuning or a new player. Session leaders sometimes choose sets to keep one key centre for whistle and pipes.

Ornament wars happen when two strong players with different regional accents duel on the same reel — thrilling when balanced, exhausting when competitive. The genres lesson cannot settle those arguments; it can teach you to name what you hear and choose your battles.

Style checklist for learners

  1. Identify the form before you tap your foot — reel versus jig first, then hornpipe versus reel.
  2. Learn one regional recording deeply before mixing ornaments from three counties.
  3. Play sets, not isolated tunes — transitions and endings are social skills.
  4. Separate airs from dance music in programming unless the room invites song.
  5. Respect song context — political and emigration ballads carry living memory.
  6. Lilt a tune before ornamenting it on your instrument — voice teaches pulse.
