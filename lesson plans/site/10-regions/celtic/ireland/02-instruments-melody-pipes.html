Irish Instruments — Melody and Pipes

Track: regions | Tier: 2 | Difficulty: 4/10 Prerequisites: regions-celtic-ireland-01-overview Tags: ireland, fiddle, flute, whistle, pipes, harp

Overview

Melody instruments lead the tune in most sessions. This lesson covers the five core melodic voices: fiddle, flute, tin whistle, uilleann pipes, and Celtic harp — their role, typical keys, and how they sit in the texture.

Irish fiddle

The Irish fiddle is the same instrument as the violin but played with trad bowing, ornamentation, and intonation habits. It is the most common session melody instrument in many regions (especially the west and midlands).

Players use slurs, rolls, trebles, and regional bow patterns. Open-string drones and slightly narrowed thirds are common. Fiddle repertoire overlaps with flute and whistle — same tunes, different articulation.

Session role: Lead melody, harmony drones on open strings, driving reels and jigs at dance tempo.

Irish flute and tin whistle

The Irish flute is usually a simple-system wooden (or modern composite) flute in D, with a breathy, reedy tone. The tin whistle (penny whistle) is a six-holed fipple flute — cheap, portable, and ubiquitous for learners and masters alike.

Both are diatonic in D but use cross-fingerings for accidentals. Whistle players often start young; flute demands more breath support. Many players double on both.

Session role: Melody, often paired with fiddle in unison or harmony. Whistle excels in tight ornament; flute carries longer lyrical phrases.

Uilleann pipes

Uilleann pipes (píobaí uilleann, “pipes of the elbow”) are Ireland’s national bagpipe. Unlike Highland pipes, they use elbow bellows (not mouth-blown), a closed-end chanter for staccato, and often regulators — keyed pipes for chords.

The chanter spans two octaves with chromatic possibilities. Volume suits indoor performance. Concert pitch is typically D.

Session role: Solo melody, slow airs, and set dances; less common in loud pub sessions than fiddle but iconic in Irish sound.

Celtic harp

The Celtic harp (cláirseach) is a wire- or nylon-strung harp with a tradition dating to medieval Gaelic courts. Historical harpers were honoured professionals; the instrument declined and was revived in the 20th century.

Modern trad harpists play dance tunes, airs, and song accompaniment. Bass wires and damping technique create a bell-like resonance distinct from classical pedal harp.

Session role: Melody or accompaniment; often featured in concerts and recordings rather than noisy pub sessions.

Quiz Questions

Q1. Uilleann pipes are inflated primarily by:

  • A) Elbow bellows strapped to the player
  • B) Mouth blowing only, like Highland pipes
  • C) An electric pump
  • D) Foot treadles on the floor

Answer: A

Q2. The tin whistle and Irish flute are most commonly associated with which home key?

  • A) D
  • B) B-flat concert band key only
  • C) F-sharp only
  • D) No standard key — fully atonal

Answer: A

Q3. Which instrument is typically the most common melody voice in many Irish pub sessions?

  • A) Irish fiddle
  • B) Lambeg drum
  • C) Synthesizer
  • D) Timpani

Answer: A

Q4. Uilleann pipe chanter staccato is possible because the chanter end is closed.

Answer: True

Q5. The Celtic harp (cláirseach) has no historical connection to Gaelic Ireland.

Answer: False — Harpers were documented in Irish law and annals for centuries.

Q6. Irish flute tone is often described as:

  • A) Breathier and more reedy than a classical silver flute
  • B) Identical to a Baroque trumpet
  • C) Purely electronic
  • D) Produced only by striking strings

Answer: A

Q7. Regulators on uilleann pipes are used for:

  • A) Harmonic accompaniment via keyed pipes
  • B) Tuning the chanter only
  • C) Storing spare reeds
  • D) Controlling stage lighting

Answer: A

Q8. Tin whistle and Irish flute players often learn the same tune repertoire as fiddlers.

Answer: True

Q9. Highland bagpipes and uilleann pipes use the same mouth-blown inflation system.

Answer: False — Uilleann pipes use bellows; Highland pipes are mouth-blown.

Q10. In a loud session, harp is sometimes less practical than fiddle or whistle because:

  • A) It is quieter and more easily masked by other instruments
  • B) It cannot play in any key
  • C) It is illegal in Ireland
  • D) It has no strings

Answer: A
