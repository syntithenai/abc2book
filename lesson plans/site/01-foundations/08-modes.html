Modes: Dorian, Mixolydian, and Beyond

Track: foundations | Difficulty: 4/10 | Time: ~12 min Prerequisites: Major Scales, Minor Scales Tags: modes, dorian, mixolydian

Overview

Modes are scales formed by starting on each degree of the major scale. Seven modes — Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian — predate major/minor tonality and remain central to folk, jazz, and modal composition.

The seven modes

Ionian = major. Aeolian = natural minor. Dorian: minor 3rd, major 6th (D dorian: D E F G A B C D). Mixolydian: major 3rd, minor 7th (G mixolydian: G A B C D E F G). Phrygian: minor 2nd. Lydian: raised 4th. Locrian: diminished 5th — rare as tonal centre.

Modes in folk music

Irish tunes often centre on D with C natural (D mixolydian) or A with G natural (A dorian). Players describe these as ‘major with a flat 7’ or ‘minor with a major 6’ without Greek names. Modes explain why certain accidentals persist across a tune.

Modal harmony

Do not sharp the characteristic flat note to fit major-key thinking. D mixolydian wants C natural, not C♯. Harmonise with chords built from the mode’s scale degrees: I in mixolydian is a dominant-seventh-type chord (G7 in G mixolydian).

Parallel vs relative

D dorian is relative to C major (same notes, different centre). D dorian is parallel to D minor (same tonic, different sixth and seventh).

Quiz Questions

Q1. D dorian differs from D natural minor by:

  • A) Major sixth (B natural)
  • B) Lowered third
  • C) No fourth degree
  • D) Extra sharp on every note

Answer: A

Q2. Mixolydian differs from major by:

  • A) Lowered seventh
  • B) Lowered third
  • C) Absent fifth
  • D) 6/8 meter only

Answer: A

Q3. D dorian shares key signature with C major.

Answer: True — Same pitch collection, different tonal centre.
