Natural, Harmonic, and Melodic Minor

Track: foundations | Difficulty: 2/10 | Time: ~10 min Prerequisites: Major Scales Tags: scales, minor, modes

Overview

Minor keys share the tonic–dominant anchor of major but use different pitch collections. Three minor variants — natural, harmonic, and melodic — explain most minor-key accidentals in repertoire from Bach to Irish sessions.

Natural minor (Aeolian)

Pattern: W-H-W-W-H-W-W. A natural minor: A B C D E F G A. Same key signature as C major — A is the relative minor of C, found a minor third below C. Natural minor has no leading tone; the seventh is a whole step below the tonic.

Harmonic minor

Raises the seventh degree by a semitone: A harmonic minor = A B C D E F G♯ A. The raised seventh creates a leading tone, enabling strong V→i cadences. Common in classical harmony and Eastern European folk. The augmented second between ♭6 and ♯7 (F to G♯ in A minor) gives harmonic minor its distinctive colour.

Melodic minor

Ascending: raises 6 and 7 (A B C D E F♯ G♯ A). Descending: often reverts to natural minor (A G F E D C B A), avoiding awkward intervals. Jazz treats melodic minor as a single scale ascending and descending — a different usage worth noting.

In trad music

Tunes in A minor, E minor, or D minor frequently mix flavours: dorian sixth against aeolian seventh, or raised seventh only at cadences. Listen for which accidentals appear and where.

Quiz Questions

Q1. What defines harmonic minor vs natural minor?

  • A) Raised seventh degree
  • B) Lowered third
  • C) Raised fifth
  • D) All degrees lowered

Answer: A — Only the seventh is raised, creating a leading tone.

Q2. A natural minor shares a key signature with C major.

Answer: True — They are relative major and minor.

Q3. The interval between ♭6 and ♯7 in harmonic minor is:

  • A) Augmented second (three semitones)
  • B) Minor second
  • C) Perfect fourth
  • D) Major third

Answer: A — This augmented second is harmonic minor’s characteristic colour.
