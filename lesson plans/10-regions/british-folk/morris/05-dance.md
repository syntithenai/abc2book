## Core material

Cotswold, Border, Northwest morris

Study **morris and country dance** through listening first. Wikipedia's article on [Morris dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_dance) orients geography and history; recordings prove how musicians phrase ornaments, tempo, and ensemble balance in real rooms.

### Regional accent and social context

Tradition lives in **community practice** — festivals, dances, teaching networks, and archives — not only on concert stages. For **British Isles Folk**, ask who leads (voice, fiddle, pipes, percussion), whether dance drives repertoire, and how revival institutions shaped what you hear on modern recordings.

### Instruments and voices in this lesson

Lead voices: **melodeon, pipe and tabor, fiddle**. Ensemble layer: **morris side band, concertina**. Compare timbre and role with the anchor unit before assuming techniques transfer unchanged.

### Forms, dance, and rhythm

Named forms include **jigs, polkas, processional tunes**. Dance context: **Cotswold, Border, Northwest morris**. Metre and foot-tap feel are not decorative — they explain why musicians accent certain beats and phrase repeats.

### History, transmission, and today

Historical threads: **Sharp revival, Morris Ring**. Contemporary scenes: **display sides, May Day**. Tune repertoire anchor: **Banbury Bill, Lads-a-Bunchum**. Follow lesson playlists and reading lists for recorded examples.

### Listening habits

1. Queue one field or archive recording and one contemporary ensemble track.
2. Log metre, lead instrument, ornament, and social context in four columns.
3. Return to the parallel anchor lesson if comparison feels abstract.
