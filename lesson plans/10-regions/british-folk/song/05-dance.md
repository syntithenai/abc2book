## Core material

social song, club performance

Study **english song tradition** through listening first. Wikipedia's article on [English folk music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_folk_music) orients geography and history; recordings prove how musicians phrase ornaments, tempo, and ensemble balance in real rooms.

### Regional accent and social context

Tradition lives in **community practice** — festivals, dances, teaching networks, and archives — not only on concert stages. For **British Isles Folk**, ask who leads (voice, fiddle, pipes, percussion), whether dance drives repertoire, and how revival institutions shaped what you hear on modern recordings.

### Instruments and voices in this lesson

Lead voices: **unaccompanied voice, concertina, guitar**. Ensemble layer: **piano, harmony singers**. Compare timbre and role with the anchor unit before assuming techniques transfer unchanged.

### Forms, dance, and rhythm

Named forms include **ballad, broadside, shanty, carol**. Dance context: **social song, club performance**. Metre and foot-tap feel are not decorative — they explain why musicians accent certain beats and phrase repeats.

### History, transmission, and today

Historical threads: **Child ballads, broadsides, Topic Records**. Contemporary scenes: **folk clubs, radio ballads**. Tune repertoire anchor: **Barbara Allen, The Cruel Ship's Carpenter**. Follow lesson playlists and reading lists for recorded examples.

### Listening habits

1. Queue one field or archive recording and one contemporary ensemble track.
2. Log metre, lead instrument, ornament, and social context in four columns.
3. Return to the parallel anchor lesson if comparison feels abstract.
4. Avoid generic world-music compilations when building ear training.
