# Breton Traditional Instruments — Traditional Voices I

**Track:** regions | **Tier:** 2 | **Difficulty:** 4/10
**Prerequisites:** regions-celtic-brittany-01-overview
**Tags:** brittany, bombard, binou, biniou, fiddle, harp, sonerion, fest-noz

## Overview

This lesson examines four instruments at the heart of Breton traditional music: the **bombard**, **binou kozh** (old-style Breton bagpipe), **fiddle**, and **Celtic harp**. Fest-noz programmes and bagad stages present them as "melody leaders" — useful shorthand that hides overlap. A bombard player drives rhythm with articulation; a harpist may harmonise a **kan ha diskan** set or step into dance melody at a fest-noz; fiddlers in **Trégor** and **Léon** share repertoire with pipers but phrase for dancers, not adjudicators.

The four voices share diatonic dance repertoire, ornament learned by ear, and association with regional fest-noz circuits. They differ in breath versus bow, in outdoor versus hall volume, and in whether they pair as **sonerion** duet or stand alone. A Cornouaille **gavotte** led by bombard–binou is not a Vannetais **plinn** led by accordion — regional accent matters from the first lesson.

Read alongside **Session Voices II** (accordion, guitar, **bagad**), which covers equally essential fest-noz and competition voices.


### Famous players and memorable details

**Alan Stivell** (born 1944) placed Breton harp on global stages; **bodadeg** sonerien keep bombard-binou duets alive in fest-noz culture. **Memorable detail:** Breton pipes are **paired** (bombard + biniou) — not a solo Highland pipe on a hill.

## Compared with Ireland

Irish trad's core melody stack is fiddle, flute, whistle, and uilleann pipes in pub sessions. Breton trad's emblematic pair is **bombard and binou** — both in B-flat folk tuning, piercing enough for unamplified dance halls. Irish uilleann pipes use bellows, regulators, and session etiquette favouring indoor volume control; Breton **sonerion** favours **outdoor projection** and **unison doubling** with the bombard.

| Voice | Irish parallel | Breton distinction |
|-------|---------------|-------------------|
| Bombard | Folk oboe cousins; not uilleann chanter | Paired with binou; fest-noz lead |
| Binou | Uilleann pipes (superficial bagpipe kin) | One-armed pipe; sharper, smaller scale |
| Fiddle | Irish fiddle | Dance tempo for gavotte/plinn, not reel sets |
| Harp | Cláirseach / Celtic harp revival | Stivell lineage; concert and song |

## Bombard

The **bombard** (*talabost* in Breton) is a loud folk oboe, typically in B-flat, with a penetrating timbre that defines **sonerion** bands. Wikipedia describes it as a traditional Breton woodwind paired with the **biniou** for fest-noz and parade music. It is **not** a medieval weapon name misapplied — the instrument's volume answered the need to lead dancers in barns and fields before PA systems.

### Technique and cultural context

Bombard players use **circular breathing** or staggered breathing between partners so the dance pulse does not break. Fingering resembles other European folk oboes; ornament is **articulation-heavy** — tongued patterns that match **gavotte** lift. Intonation is negotiated with the binou player; fixed temperament purity is less important than **blend**.

Volume is the bombard's social role: it cuts through crowd noise where a fiddle might sink. That power demands **floor awareness** — blasting during a lyrical **son** vocal section is a fest-noz faux pas.

### Fest-noz and bagad role

In classic **Léon** and **Cornouaille** lineups, bombard **leads melody** in unison with binou. In **bagad** ensembles, a **bombard section** doubles pipe melodies at competition scale. Some contemporary fest-noz bands fold bombard into larger horn arrangements; tradition-focused nights keep the pair central.

[[entity:bombard]] players often learn through **bodadeg** classes and mentor relationships rather than conservatory tracks.

### Famous players and memorable details

**Jorj Botuhel** and generations of **sonerion** teachers standardised pedagogy through **Sonerion** publications. **Bagad** competition recordings document regional bombard sections — Kemper, Bro Kemperle, and Brieg bagads export reference timbres.

**Memorable detail:** A bombard beginner's first challenge is not finger speed but **endurance** — dance sets run longer than concert pieces, and partners depend on steady pulse.

## Binou kozh (Breton bagpipe)

The **binou kozh** ("old bagpipe") is the small Breton bagpipe, pitched high and bright, traditionally paired with bombard. It uses a **single octave** scale in folk practice; the **one-armed** drone layout differs from Scottish Highland pipes and from Irish uilleann pipes' bellows-and-regulator system.

### Technique and cultural context

Binou players maintain **constant bag pressure** and tune to bombard partners. The instrument's **shrill** register projects outdoors — bagad parades, festival openings, village processions. Indoor fest-noz use demands sensitivity; a binou beside a fiddle-heavy band needs balance.

Repertoire overlaps **gavotte**, **mazurka**, and **son** melodies shared across Brittany; Cornouaille tune names dominate export playlists.

### Fest-noz and bagad role

**Sonerion** duet with bombard is the classic fest-noz lead. **Bagad** format multiplies binou players into **sections** with choreographed marching. Competition **suites** — including material related to the **Suite Sudarmoricaine** — showcase massed pipe tone.

[[entity:binou]] appears in entity markers alongside bombard in tune books and fest-noz posters.

### Historical notes

Revival movements from the 1950s onward rebuilt pipe making and teaching after rural depopulation and wartime disruption. **Bagad** creation in the mid-twentieth century gave binou a **youth pipeline** that pub-based sessions never provided in Ireland's model.

## Fiddle

The Breton **fiddle** shares the violin's physical form but learns **gavotte** and **plinn** pulse, not primarily reel session drive. **Trégor** and **Léon** fiddlers trade tunes with pipers; **Cornouaille** bands may feature fiddle as secondary melody behind sonerion.

### Technique and regional accent

Bow patterns emphasise **dance lift** — shorter strokes on **plinn**, steadier drive on **gavotte**. Ornament includes cuts and bowed pulses familiar to Irish fiddlers, but **tempo discipline** follows the floor. Irish-style roll-heavy Sligo reels are the wrong template for a Vannetais walking dance.

### Fest-noz role

Fiddle often **doubles** or **fills** when bombard–binou rests; in accordion-led southeast bands it may lead. Twin fiddling appears in some fest-noz recordings; unison with pipes is more common in the northwest.

### Listening guide

Compare fiddle-led **Trégor** recordings with bombard-led **Cornouaille** sets of the same tune name if available — accent differs even when notes match.

## Celtic harp

The **Breton Celtic harp** revival intertwines with **Alan Stivell**, who popularised harp, **binou**, and fusion staging from the late 1960s. Stivell's father **Jord Cochevelou** built harps that helped spark the **renaissance** documented on albums like *Renaissance de la harpe celtique* (1972).

### Technique and cultural context

Harp in Brittany spans **classical Celtic harp** technique, **modal** dance accompaniment, and **rock-fusion** arrangements. Unlike Irish harp's historical **cláirseach** lineage alone, Breton revival harp is inseparable from **fest-noz song** and **concert export**.

### Concert and fest-noz role

At fest-noz, harp is less common than bombard or accordion as dance lead — but harp supports **kan ha diskan** and song programmes. On festival main stages, [[entity:stivell]]'s lineage defines public imagination of "Breton harp."

### Famous players

**Alan Stivell** remains the reference — harp, pipes, and political-cultural symbolism of Breton identity. Contemporary harpists bridge fest-noz workshops and **Festival Interceltique** concerts.

## Reading and listening list

- **Sonerion** tune books — bombard–binou repertoire in standard notation and Breton names.
- **Bagad Kemper** competition sets — massed binou timbre.
- **Alan Stivell** — *Renaissance de la harpe celtique* — harp revival context.
- **Dastum** field recordings — village sonerion before stage amplification.

## Key points

- Bombard and binou are the emblematic fest-noz pair — volume, unison, and endurance define their role.
- Binou differs from Irish uilleann pipes in scale, social pairing, and bagad massed format.
- Fiddle serves dance pulse in gavotte and plinn — not Irish reel session defaults.
- Harp revival links to Stivell and concert stages as much as village halls.
- Compared with Ireland: paired winds replace fiddle-flute session centre; dance floor sets tempo.
