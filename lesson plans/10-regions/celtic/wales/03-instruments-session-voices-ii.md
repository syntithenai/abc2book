## Overview

This lesson covers **guitar**, **accordion**, and the **contemporary folk ensemble** — the voices that complete Welsh twmpath bands, folk clubs, and festival stages. If Traditional Voices I taught harp, fiddle, and ancient winds, Session Voices II teaches the **rhythm-harmony engine** and the **post-1970 revival band** that blended rock, reggae, and country with alawon gwerin (folk tunes).

Welsh ensemble culture differs from Irish pub sessions: a **caller** may need steady chord changes for dancers; a **folk-rock** band may amplify for a tent crowd. Guitar and accordion are not "background only" — they **start tunes**, **signal sets**, and **define regional accent** in south Wales clubs especially.


### Ensemble voices

Welsh folk clubs favour **guitar and accordion** in twmpath bands rather than Irish pub stacks of fiddle-flute-box. **Calan** represents the contemporary ensemble model — tight arrangements, festival energy, bilingual presentation.

## Compared with Ireland

Ireland's Session Voices II covers **bodhrán, guitar, bouzouki, banjo, box accordion, and concertina** — the pub session rhythm stack. Wales shares guitar and accordion but uses them primarily for **twmpath dance** and **folk-club song** rather than reel-and-jig marathon sets.

## Guitar in Welsh folk

The **guitar** entered Welsh folk through **skiffle**, **American country**, **chapel harmony singing**, and the **1970s revival**. It provides **chordal backing** for twmpath waltzes and hornpipes, **fingerstyle** introductions to songs, and **electric rhythm** in fusion bands.

### Technique and repertoire

Folk guitarists favour **open chords** in G, D, and A; **capo** use matches singers' keys. Welsh song accompaniment respects **hymn harmonic** language — plagal cadences and modal touches from chapel tradition. Dance backing requires **steady tempo** — dancers punish rushing during a **plygi** set.

### Notable contexts

**Folk clubs** from Cardiff to Aberystwyth feature solo guitar–voice acts. Revival bands layered electric guitar over fiddle and harp (Lesson 7). Unlike Irish bouzouki culture (Planxty lineage), Welsh guitar often stays **straightforward rhythmic** — serving dance and song rather than virtuoso counter-melody.

**Memorable detail:** Many Welsh folk guitarists learned **chords in chapel** before learning hornpipes — hymnody as harmonic boot camp.

## Accordion in Welsh folk

The **accordion** — button or piano — is the **workhorse** of twmpath bands alongside fiddle. It carries **melody** on dance sets, supplies **bass-chord** accompaniment, and cuts through hall acoustics where harp might be drowned.

### Technique and style

Welsh accordionists phrase **hornpipes** with dotted bounce and **waltzes** with lilt. Button accordion **B/C** or **C#/D** layouts appear depending on player background; piano accordion dominates some south Wales clubs. Ornament is bellows-driven: **crisp attacks** on plygi downbeats.

### Social role

At a **twmpath**, accordion and fiddle **share melody** — trading parts or playing in unison. The accordion often **starts** a set because its volume gathers the room. Eisteddfod accordion categories prize **clean articulation** and **repertoire depth**.

**Memorable detail:** A twmpath without accordion is possible but unusual in south and mid Wales — the instrument is as central as fiddle in many bands.

## Contemporary folk ensemble

The **contemporary Welsh folk ensemble** spans acoustic twmpath bands (fiddle, accordion, guitar, optional harp) to **folk-rock** groups like [[entity:bob-delyn]] — fiddle, harp, guitar, bass, drums, and vocals in Welsh and Breton.

### Twmpath band lineup

Classic format: **fiddle** + **accordion** melody, **guitar** chords, optional **harp** for penillion or slow airs, **caller** on microphone. Rhythm comes from **dancers' feet** (clog) and steady chord pulse — not bodhrán-centric.

### Folk-rock and festival band

Post-1990 revival acts added **electric guitar**, **bass**, **drums**, and **world music** influences (reggae, country). [[entity:bob-delyn]] fronted by **Twm Morys** (Chief Bard / *Prifardd*) blended trad tunes with rock staging at **National Eisteddfod** concerts and **Sain** label releases. Festival culture (Lesson 7) depends on these ensembles to fill main stages.

### Folk clubs as transmission hubs

**Folk clubs** (*clybiau gwerin*) host guest nights, learners' sessions, and twmpath fundraisers. They are Wales's closest cousin to Irish pub sessions — but **programmed** (acts, intervals, callers) more often than open-ended tune marathons.

## How guitar and accordion fit with harp and fiddle

Picture **Saturday twmpath**: accordion launches hornpipe set, fiddle joins at chorus, guitar holds G–D–Em–C pattern, dancers clog on wooden floor. Picture **Sunday folk club**: solo guitar introduces harpist for penillion demonstration.

Your homework: compare a **twmpath recording** (acoustic trio) with a **Bob Delyn** track (amplified ensemble). Note **tempo authority** — who leads? — and **arrangement density**.

## Key points

- **Accordion** is central to twmpath dance bands — melody and bass-chord drive.
- **Guitar** provides harmonic backing for dance and song; chapel harmony influences chord choices.
- Welsh ensemble culture is **dance- and club-programmed**, not open session marathon by default.
- Contemporary **folk-rock** bands extend trad repertoire to festival stages.
- Compared with Ireland: shared guitar/accordion but different bodhrán role; bouzouki less canonical.
- Folk clubs and twmpath bands are primary modern transmission for these voices.
