# Welsh Traditional Music — History, Revival, and Transmission

**Track:** regions | **Tier:** 6 | **Difficulty:** 8/10
**Prerequisites:** regions-celtic-wales-05-dance
**Tags:** wales, chapel, industrial, revival, transmission, collectors, eisteddfod, 1970s

## Overview

Why does a Welsh hymn tune fill a rugby stadium while a plygi lives in a village hall? Because Welsh traditional music survived language pressure, industrial upheaval, and emigration through **chapel**, **eisteddfod**, **collectors**, and **revival media** — not through a single pub-session pipeline. This lesson follows transmission from medieval harp law to twentieth-century **folk clubs**, **Sain** recordings, and the **1970s folk revival** that fed Bob Delyn and festival culture (Lesson 7).

You have met tune types, instruments, and dance. Here you learn **how repertoire moved** — and why chapel harmony and coal-valley migration matter as much as harp manuscripts.

## Compared with Ireland

Ireland's history lesson (`regions-celtic-ireland-06-history`) traces annals, Carolan, O'Neill's Chicago tune books, Comhaltas 1951, and diaspora 78s. Wales parallels **collector print** and **revival institutions** but emphasises **Nonconformist chapel**, **industrial south** migration, and **eisteddfod** rather than fleadh session culture.

## Medieval harp and bardic law

Welsh law codes (e.g. **Hywel Dda** traditions) and poetry treat harpers as **privileged professionals**. The **crwth** and harp served courts and gentry before fiddle democratisation. **Bardic** praise poetry (*cywydd*, *englyn*) linked verse to musical performance — ancestor of **penillion** discipline.

Loss of aristocratic patronage after Tudor conquest and Anglicisation pushed musicians toward **village** and **chapel** contexts — same broad pattern as Irish harp decline, different legal and linguistic details.

## Chapel influence: hymnody as national spine

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century **Nonconformist revival** (*Cymru yn deffro* — Wales awakes) made **chapel** the centre of Welsh musical education. Congregations sang **hymn tunes** in harmony; **Cymanfa Ganu** festivals gathered thousands. Composers like **William Williams Pantycelyn** (hymn writer) and tune writers **John Hughes** (*Cwm Rhondda*) embedded melody in daily life.

[[entity:chapel]] culture taught:

- **Four-part harmony** literacy in working-class communities
- **Male voice choirs** in mining valleys — export to diaspora
- **Bilingual** repertoire — Welsh lyrics, sometimes English gospel hymns

Folk dance did not die in chapel disapproval everywhere — but **Sunday sacred** and **Saturday twmpath** split social time, shaping who played where. Revivalists in the 1970s **reclaimed** Saturday night without abandoning hymn heritage.

**Memorable detail:** *Cwm Rhondda* was sung at **Princess Diana's funeral** (1997) — Welsh hymn tune as global ceremonial music.

## Industrial south Wales: coal, steel, and migration

**Industrial south Wales** — Rhondda, Merthyr, Swansea valleys — concentrated population, **choirs**, and **pub folk** in tight communities. Coal and steel work brought **internal migration** from Welsh-speaking north and west; English became dominant in some valleys while music kept Welsh titles alive.

Transmission paths:

- **Eisteddfod** competitions in mining towns
- **St David's Day** school concerts
- **Emigration** to Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Australia — choirs and harp societies abroad
- **BBC Wales** broadcasting folk and hymn programmes post-1922

Industrial decline in late twentieth century closed chapels and halls — threatening venues — but also fuelled **folk revival activism** to document survivors.

## Collectors and print tradition

Before recording, **print** fixed tunes:

- **Nicholas Bennett** — *Alawon Fy Ngwlad* (1896) — foundational Welsh tune collection
- **J. Lloyd Williams** and **Arthur Somerville Velarde** — *Alawon Cymru* and related editions
- **Maria Jane Williams** — *Ancient National Airs of Gwent and Morgannwg* (1844)

Collectors mediated **oral** village repertoire into **sheet music** for middle-class pianos and eisteddfod competitors — same tension as O'Neill in Chicago: **standard settings** versus living variation.

## The 1970s folk revival

The **1970s folk revival** in Wales paralleled Irish and Scottish revivals: **folk clubs** multiplied, **Sain** label (founded **1969**) recorded Welsh-language rock and folk, **BBC** folk programmes archived artists. Young musicians learned **trad tunes** and **rock technique** simultaneously — pipeline to [[entity:bob-delyn]] (1988 onward).

Key dynamics:

- **Political Welsh language** activism (*Cymdeithas yr Iaith*) linked folk to identity
- **Festivals** (National Eisteddfod, Cambridge Folk Festival Welsh acts) exported sound
- **Teachers** standardised twmpath dance and harp pedagogy

Compared with Irish **Comhaltas 1951** foundation, Welsh revival stayed **more fragmented** across clubs, labels, and eisteddfod — no single global body, but [[entity:eisteddfod]] prestige anchor.

**Memorable detail:** Sain's first hit (*Y Blew* by **Heather Jones**, 1973) proved Welsh-language pop could sell — folk rode the same infrastructure.

## Broadcasting, archives, and modern transmission

**BBC Radio Cymru** (1982) and **BBC Radio Wales** sustain folk playlists. **St Fagans National Museum of History** preserves instruments and field recordings. **Trac Cymru** funds touring and education. **YouTube** and social media now spread twmpath clips — oral tradition with digital speed.

Debates echo Ireland: **competition precision** versus **club swing**; **language policy** versus **bilingual audiences**; **fusion** versus **source**.

## Transmission today: who carries the tune?

| Carrier | Role |
|---------|------|
| Eisteddfod tutors | Standardise harp, fiddle, song |
| Folk club organisers | Book acts, host twmpath |
| Male voice choirs | Hymn repertoire, hwyl |
| Diaspora societies | St David's Day, choirs abroad |
| Labels (Sain, Fflach) | Document revival generations |

## Key points

- **Chapel hymnody** trained Wales in harmony and communal singing — foundation for choirs and hwyl.
- **Industrial south** concentrated choirs, eisteddfod life, and bilingual tension.
- **Collectors** like Nicholas Bennett printed oral repertoire for national audience.
- **1970s revival** linked folk clubs, Sain label, and language activism.
- Compared with Ireland: chapel over Gaeltacht; eisteddfod over Comhaltas monoculture.
- Modern transmission mixes eisteddfod teaching, folk clubs, and digital media.
