# Welsh Instruments — Traditional Voices I

**Track:** regions | **Tier:** 2 | **Difficulty:** 4/10
**Prerequisites:** regions-celtic-wales-01-overview
**Tags:** wales, fiddle, triple-harp, pibgorn, crwth, eisteddfod, penillion

## Overview

This lesson examines four instruments at the heart of Welsh traditional music: the **fiddle**, **triple harp** (*telyn deires*), **pibgorn**, and **crwth**. Guidebooks often group them as "melody instruments," but session and eisteddfod culture rewards **listening** over labelling: a harpist voices chords while a singer performs penillion; a fiddler drives a twmpath set; a pibgorn revivalist may play solo in a small hall where an accordion would dominate.

The four voices share diatonic tune types, ornament learned by ear, and repertoire tied to dance and song. They differ in breath versus pluck, in competition stage versus folk club, and in revival status — the triple harp never left living hands, while crwth and pibgorn required twentieth-century reconstruction. Read alongside **Session Voices II**, which covers guitar, accordion, and contemporary ensemble voices.

## Compared with Ireland

Ireland's Traditional Voices I lesson centres **fiddle, flute, whistle, uilleann pipes, and cláirseach** — the pub session stack. Wales swaps flute-and-pipes prominence for **triple harp** as national melody voice and preserves **pibgorn** and **crwth** as ancient cousins rather than daily session tools.

| Bowed strings | Fiddle (default leader) | Fiddle (twmpath and folk club) |

## Session and stage roles overlap

| Situation | What you might hear |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Twmpath in a village hall | Fiddle and accordion on melody, harp resting until penillion set |
| Eisteddfod folk harp competition | Triple harp solo; fiddle and pibgorn in separate categories |

**Overlap in practice:** Fiddle leads dance sets; harp leads song and competition repertoire; pibgorn and crwth appear in revival and education contexts more than weekly pub nights.

## Welsh fiddle

The **Welsh fiddle** is the violin played with traditional bowing and ornament in folk clubs, twmpath bands, and eisteddfod fiddle categories. Wales shares reel, hornpipe, and waltz repertoire with England and Ireland, but **accent** — bow pressure, drone use, and tune choice — reflects local collectors and family transmission rather than Sligo rolls or Donegal snap.

### Technique and ornament

Welsh fiddlers hold standard violin posture but use **cuts**, **rolls**, and **slides** in a folk vocabulary related to Irish and Scottish neighbours. **Vibrato** appears sparingly outside slow airs. Open-string **drone** colour suits modal tunes and hymn-derived melodies. Players learn from twmpath band leaders, eisteddfod tutors, and recordings of artists like **Patrick Rimes** and **Cass Meurig** (who also plays crwth).

### Social role

In **twmpath** bands the fiddle often **starts dance sets** alongside accordion — driving tempo for callers. Eisteddfod adjudicators prize **clean tone** and **steady rhythm**; folk clubs tolerate more **swing**. A sensitive fiddler drops volume when harp takes a penillion tune or when a singer performs *Ar Lan y Môr*.

### Regional notes

**North Wales** fiddlers share repertoire with harpists — *Cader Idris*, *Dafydd y Garreg Wen*. **Mid Wales** folk clubs maintain mixed instrument sessions. **South Wales** revival bands blend fiddle with guitar and rock instrumentation (see Lesson 7).

**Memorable detail:** Welsh fiddle rarely dominates global "Celtic fiddle" marketing the way Sligo does — which frees learners from one template but requires active regional listening.

## Triple harp (telyn deires)

The **triple harp** is Wales's signature instrument: three parallel rows of strings (two outer diatonic rows, inner chromatic row) played with **fingernails**, selective damping, and left-hand patterns that support **penillion** singers. 
### Famous players and memorable details

**Nansi Richards** (1888–1979), *Brenhines y Delyn* (Queen of the Harp), performed penillion and folk sets when triple harp seemed endangered. **Llio Rhydderch** continues the tradition with contemporary compositions and eisteddfod teaching. **Memorable detail:** Richards played for royalty and chapel alike — harp prestige in Wales spans sacred and competitive stages.

[[entity:triple-harp]] appears on eisteddfod stages, folk club nights, and recordings from **Nansi Richards** ("The Queen of the Harp," 1888–1979) to **Llio Rhydderch**, **Elinor Bennett**, and **Rhian Samuel**.

### Technique

Harpists sit with the instrument on the **right shoulder** (Welsh orientation, opposite to many Irish harp traditions). **Plucking** is bright and articulate; **harmonics** and **trills** decorate slow airs. The chromatic inner row allows modulations impossible on a single-row folk harp without retuning. Competition repertoire includes traditional airs, dance tune arrangements, and composed pieces.

### Penillion partnership

In **penillion**, the harp plays a **fixed melody** (*tune* / *alaw*); the singer weaves a **counter-melody** in strict rhythm. The harpist must keep tempo steady while leaving space for verbal brilliance — a duet discipline unlike Irish session tune-chaining.

### Eisteddfod and revival

Harp competitions at [[entity:eisteddfod]] elevated technical standards nationwide. Makers and teachers in **Llanrwst**, **Bangor**, and Cardiff keep instruments in working order. UNESCO has not inscribed triple harp separately, but Welsh institutions treat it as **living national heritage**.

**Memorable detail:** Nansi Richards performed for **Queen Elizabeth II's coronation tour** in 1953 — folk harp on a world stage.

## Pibgorn

The **pibgorn** (plural *pibgyrn*) is a **single-reed** pipe of ancient Wales — a wooden tube with finger holes, related to the hornpipe family of European folk winds. Historical references and iconography show it in rural Wales; the instrument nearly vanished before **twentieth-century revival** makers and players reconstructed it from museum specimens and oral memory.

### Technique and sound

The pibgorn produces a **reedy, pastoral** tone suited to slow airs and dance tunes in smaller rooms. Players control breath and embouchure on a single reed; ornament uses finger articulation and rhythmic tonguing. Volume is modest compared to fiddle or accordion — ideal for workshops and historical demonstrations.

### Revival context

Ensembles like **Bragod** (Bob Evans and Mary-Anne Roberts) research and perform medieval and early Welsh music including pibgorn and crwth. [[entity:pibgorn]] today signals **historical depth** in concert programmes more than twmpath dance floors — though revivalists occasionally join mixed folk bands.

![Pibgorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Pibgorn.jpg?width=640)

**Memorable detail:** The pibgorn's name connects to *pib* (pipe) and *corn* (horn) — Wales's answer to pastoral bagpipe cousins, without the Highland volume.

## Crwth

The **crwth** (pronounced "crooth") is a **bowed lyre** — flat bridge, few strings, historical bridge between bardic Wales and modern fiddle tradition. Medieval and Tudor references describe crwth players as professional musicians; the instrument declined as violin family instruments spread.

### Technique and historical role

The crwth is held against the chest or shoulder; the player **bows** melody strings while others may drone. Tuning and technique were reconstructed from **Welsh manuscripts** and comparative study with other European bowed lyres. [[entity:crwth]] appears in museum collections (e.g. **St Fagans National Museum of History**) and in revival performances by scholars and multi-instrumentalists like **Cass Meurig**.

### Living tradition status

Unlike triple harp, crwth is **not** a weekly twmpath instrument — it represents **archaeological revival**: proof that Welsh music predates modern fiddle and harp forms. Hearing crwth beside fiddle clarifies how repertoire **migrated** across instrument technologies.

![Crwth in museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Crwth.jpg?width=640)

**Memorable detail:** The crwth player in historical Wales could be a **professional** with legal privileges — music as craft, not hobby.

## How these four voices fit together

Picture an **eisteddfod** afternoon: triple harp competition in the pavilion, fiddle class in the tent next door. Picture a **folk club**: fiddle and harp duet for *Cader Idris*, then a Bragod member demonstrating pibgorn. None owns a fixed "melody only" slot.

Your homework: one recording each featuring Richards or Rhydderch (harp), a twmpath fiddle band, and a Bragod pibgorn track. Note **volume**, **ornament**, and **partnership** with voice. Then continue to **Session Voices II** for guitar and accordion.

## Key points

- **Triple harp** is Wales's primary traditional melody instrument and penillion partner.
- **Fiddle** drives twmpath and folk-club dance sets with repertoire overlapping British Isles neighbours.
- **Pibgorn** and **crwth** are ancient voices revived through scholarship and performance.
- Eisteddfod competition shapes harp and fiddle standards; twmpath culture shapes dance rhythm.
- Compared with Ireland: harp over pipes; shared fiddle ornament vocabulary, different social stages.
- Instrument roles overlap — harp harmonises, fiddle drives, revival winds educate.
