## Core material

rubab, sitar, voice

Study **pakistan & afghanistan folk** through listening first. Wikipedia's article on [Music of Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_of_Pakistan) orients geography and history; recordings prove how musicians phrase ornaments, tempo, and ensemble balance in real rooms.

### Regional accent and social context

Tradition lives in **community practice** — festivals, dances, teaching networks, and archives — not only on concert stages. For **South Asian Traditions**, ask who leads (voice, fiddle, pipes, percussion), whether dance drives repertoire, and how revival institutions shaped what you hear on modern recordings.

### Instruments and voices in this lesson

Lead voices: **rubab, sitar, voice**. Ensemble layer: **tabla, ensemble**. Compare timbre and role with the anchor unit before assuming techniques transfer unchanged.

### Forms, dance, and rhythm

Named forms include **ghazal, regional folk**. Dance context: **social dance**. Metre and foot-tap feel are not decorative — they explain why musicians accent certain beats and phrase repeats.

### History, transmission, and today

Historical threads: **cross-border repertoire**. Contemporary scenes: **festivals**. Tune repertoire anchor: **Regional listening**. Follow lesson playlists and reading lists for recorded examples.

### Listening habits

1. Queue one field or archive recording and one contemporary ensemble track.
2. Log metre, lead instrument, ornament, and social context in four columns.
3. Return to the parallel anchor lesson if comparison feels abstract.
4. Avoid generic world-music compilations when building ear training.
