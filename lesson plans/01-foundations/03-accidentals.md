# Sharps, Flats, and Accidentals

**Track:** foundations | **Difficulty:** 1/10 | **Time:** ~8 min
**Prerequisites:** The Staff, Clefs, and Pitch Names
**Tags:** accidentals, pitch, keys

## Overview

Accidentals alter pitch by a semitone (half step) — the smallest interval in Western equal temperament. One fret on guitar, one piano key (white to adjacent black), or one finger hole on whistle equals one semitone.

## The three accidentals

**Sharp (♯)** raises pitch one semitone. **Flat (♭)** lowers one semitone. **Natural (♮)** cancels a previous sharp or flat for that letter name in the measure. **Double sharp (𝄪)** and **double flat (𝄫)** move two semitones — rare in folk repertoire, common in classical harmony.

## Key signatures

Sharps or flats at the start of a staff apply to every occurrence of those letter names throughout the piece unless cancelled. F♯ in the key signature means every F is sharp in every octave. This is why D major and G major feel different on the same melody shape.

## Accidentals within a measure

A sharp or flat mid-measure usually applies only until the end of that bar (in modern practice). Chromatic passing tones — notes sliding between scale degrees — appear constantly in ornamented trad playing, often unwritten. Players learn them by ear.

## Enharmonic equivalents

The same pitch can be spelled two ways: F♯ and G♭ sound identical in equal temperament. Spelling follows harmonic context — G♭ leads down to F; F♯ leads up to G.

## Quiz Questions

### Q1. What effect does a sharp have on a note?

- A) Raises pitch by one semitone
- B) Lowers by one semitone
- C) Raises by one whole tone
- D) Cancels a previous accidental

**Answer:** A — A sharp raises pitch by one half step.

### Q2. Key signature accidentals apply throughout the piece unless overridden.

**Answer:** True — They affect every occurrence of that letter name in every octave.

### Q3. F♯ and G♭ are:

- A) Enharmonic equivalents (same pitch in equal temperament)
- B) A whole tone apart
- C) Unrelated pitches
- D) Only used in minor keys

**Answer:** A — They sound the same but imply different voice-leading directions.
